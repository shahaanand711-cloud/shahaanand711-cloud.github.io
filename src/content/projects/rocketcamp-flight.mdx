## Rail-exit stability

On launch day I added 20 oz to the nose cone. Mass forward of the center of gravity moves it toward the nose, which widens the gap to the center of pressure without touching the outer geometry.

Stability is measured in calibers, the CP–CG gap divided by the reference diameter $d$:

$$
\text{caliber} = \frac{x_{CP} - x_{CG}}{d}
$$

At rail exit the simulation has $x_{CG} = 21.20$ in, $x_{CP} = 28.13$ in, and $d = 4.0$ in:

$$
\frac{28.13 - 21.20}{4.0} = 1.73
$$

Stability peaks at 2.40 calibers near burnout. What matters is the value at rail exit, where aerodynamic forces first take over from the rail.

## Why too much stability hurts

The restoring moment scales with the stability margin and the angle of attack $\alpha$.

$$
M = q \, A_{\mathrm{ref}} \, C_{N_\alpha} \, (x_{CP} - x_{CG}) \, \alpha,
\qquad q = \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2
$$

With a large margin, a crosswind at rail exit produces a strong moment that turns the nose into the wind. This is weathercocking. The flight path tilts downwind early, which adds drift and costs altitude.

The extra nose mass has a second cost. For a given thrust, heavier means slower off the rail, since $a = F_{\mathrm{net}} / m$.

## Mass over the flight

The vehicle starts at 3.342 lb and burns 0.266 lb (4.26 oz) of propellant by burnout, ending at 3.076 lb. The CG moves aft in the process, from 21.32 in to 19.93 in. After burnout, mass stays constant through touchdown.

Mass changes the acceleration at every step of the powered phase:

$$
m(t)\,\frac{dv}{dt} = T(t) - D(v, h, \alpha) - m(t)\,g(h)\sin\gamma
$$

## Deployment

The ejection charge fires at 16.4 s, which is 6.16 s after apogee. By then the rocket has fallen to 1,177 ft and is moving at 166 ft/s, so OpenRocket flags a high-speed deployment. The simulated opening load peaks at 34.5 G, and the descent slows to 28 ft/s by 57 s.
